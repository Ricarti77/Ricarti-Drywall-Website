    Spray foam or batts: which goes where, and why

    Closed-cell foam, open-cell foam, mineral wool and fibreglass batts compared for Toronto homes: where each one earns its cost, and where it does not.

    By Ricarti Drywall · 24 November 2026

    IN SHORTUse closed-cell spray foam where space is tight or moisture is a risk: rim joists, cathedral ceilings, below-grade walls. Batts, mineral wool or fibreglass, are the better value in ordinary stud walls and open attic floors. Most good houses use both.

    Most homeowners are told to choose between spray foam and batts as if it were one decision for the whole house. It is not. A well insulated house is usually a mix, and the skill is in putting each material where it works hardest for its cost.

    What each one does

    MaterialRoughly R per inchAir sealBest for
    Closed-cell spray foamR-6 to R-7YesRim joists, cathedral ceilings, basement and below-grade walls, tight cavities
    Open-cell spray foamR-3.5 to R-4YesInterior sound control, unvented roof assemblies where the design allows it
    Mineral wool battsAbout R-4NoExterior stud walls, sound walls and floors, near heat sources (it does not burn)
    Fibreglass battsR-3 to R-4NoStandard stud walls and open attic floors on a budget

    The numbers are typical ranges, not product ratings. The values your house must reach are set by your designer's energy compliance package under the Ontario Building Code, and your inspector checks against that.

    Where closed-cell foam earns its cost

    Closed-cell foam is expensive per square foot, but in a few places nothing else does the job as well. At the rim joist, where the floor framing meets the foundation, it seals the air leaks that make a basement cold and drafty. In a cathedral or sloped ceiling, where there is no attic to fill, it gives the most insulation in the least depth. On basement walls it stops warm indoor air from reaching the cold concrete, which is where condensation and mould begin.

    Foam where the house leaks or sweats. Batts where it simply needs to be warm.

    Where batts are the better buy

    In a standard 2×6 exterior wall, properly fitted batts with a continuous vapour barrier and sealed seams perform well for a fraction of the cost of foam. Fit is everything: a batt cut tight around every box and pipe, with no gaps or compression, does most of what the label promises. A batt stuffed in loosely does not.

    Mineral wool is our usual choice for walls between rooms and floors between storeys, because its density blocks sound far better than fibreglass. It also does not burn, which is why it is often used around chimneys and in fire-rated assemblies.

    The part people forget: the air barrier

    Batts slow heat, but air passes straight through them. In a cold climate the wall needs a continuous air and vapour barrier on the warm side, usually polyethylene with every seam and penetration taped or sealed. Inspectors check this before drywall goes up, and it is far cheaper to do it right then than to chase drafts later.

    What we usually recommend

    • Closed-cell foam at rim joists, sloped ceilings and below-grade walls.
    • Mineral wool or fibreglass batts in above-grade exterior walls, with a sealed vapour barrier.
    • Mineral wool in interior walls and floors where sound matters.
    • Blown-in insulation for deep open attic floors.

    Every house is different, and older Toronto homes especially. We look at the framing, the plans and your budget at the site visit and tell you where foam is worth it and where it is not.
